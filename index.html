🔄 LC Resonance - The Heart of Tuning

The tuning circuit uses an inductor (L) and variable capacitor (C) to create a resonant circuit that selectively amplifies one frequency.

Resonant Frequency:
f₀ = 1 / (2π√LC)

Where:
• L = inductance (henries)
• C = capacitance (farads)
• f₀ = resonant frequency (Hz)

By varying C with the tuning knob, you change f₀ to match different broadcast frequencies.

📡 How Radio Signals Arrive

Radio stations broadcast electromagnetic waves that induce tiny oscillating currents in the antenna.

Amplitude Modulation (AM):
s(t) = [A₀ + m(t)] × cos(2πfct)

Frequency Modulation (FM):
s(t) = A₀ × cos[2πfct + φ(t)]

• m(t) = audio message
• fc = carrier frequency

FM radio (88-108 MHz) uses frequency variation to encode audio, providing better sound quality than AM.

⚡ Impedance and Q-Factor

The resonant circuit has maximum impedance at f₀, allowing maximum voltage across it for the desired frequency.

Impedance at Resonance:
Z = R + jωL + 1/(jωC)
At ω₀: Z = R (purely resistive)

Quality Factor:
Q = f₀ / Δf = ωL/R = 1/(ωCR)

Higher Q = sharper tuning

A high Q-factor means better selectivity - the ability to separate closely spaced stations.

🔊 Superheterodyne Architecture

Modern radios use a clever intermediate frequency (IF) stage for better performance.

Frequency Conversion:
fIF = |fRF - fLO|

• fRF = received RF frequency
• fLO = local oscillator
• fIF = intermediate freq (10.7 MHz for FM)

The LO tunes with the input stage

This converts all stations to the same IF, allowing a fixed, optimized amplification and filtering stage.

🎓 Why Variable Capacitance?

The tuning knob mechanically adjusts the overlap between capacitor plates, changing C:

  • More overlap: Higher C → Lower f₀ (tune to lower frequencies)
  • Less overlap: Lower C → Higher f₀ (tune to higher frequencies)

Typical values: L ≈ 1 μH, C ≈ 10-500 pF for FM band (88-108 MHz)

Example Calculation:
For f₀ = 100 MHz with L = 1 μH:
C = 1/(4π²f₀²L) = 1/(4π² × 10¹⁶ × 10⁻⁶)
C ≈ 2.53 pF
